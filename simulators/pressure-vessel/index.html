About This Simulator

A pressure vessel is a closed container that holds a fluid at a pressure very different from the air around it: a boiler drum, an air receiver, a chemical reactor, a hydraulic cylinder. Because the wall must resist that pressure with membrane stress, the thickness is set almost entirely by three things: the pressure, the radius and the strength of the material. This simulator turns those three into a design you can see.

It has three modules. The thin-wall module finds the thickness of a cylindrical or spherical shell and compares different head shapes. The thick-wall module uses the Lamé solution to show how the hoop, radial and axial stresses change from the inside surface to the outside surface, which is necessary when the pressure is high compared with the allowable stress. The flange and bolts module sizes the bolting of a gasketed joint and checks the stress in a ring flange.

Instead of only returning numbers, the tool animates the pressure on the cross-section, colours the wall by how close it is to its limit, lets you slide a probe through a thick wall and draws every load acting on a flange. The methods are the classic ones taught in machine design and pressure vessel courses, and every material and gasket property is an input you type, so you stay in control of the data.

How to Use

  1. Step 1. Choose US or SI units. Switching converts the values you already typed.
  2. Step 2. Pick a module: thin wall, thick wall, or flange and bolts.
  3. Step 3. Enter the pressure, the radius, the allowable stress of your material at the design temperature, the joint efficiency and the corrosion allowance. Drag the pressure slider to see the wall colour and the stresses change live.
  4. Step 4. In the thin-wall module, compare the head shapes in the bar chart. In the thick-wall module, slide the probe to read the three stresses at any radius.
  5. Step 5. In the flange module, enter the gasket data (width, m and y), the allowable stresses and the flange and bolt geometry. Read the required bolt area, the loads, and the flange stress, and use the fullscreen button to inspect the load diagram.

The Method Behind the Numbers

For a thin cylindrical shell with inside radius R, allowable stress S and joint efficiency E, the thickness required by the hoop and by the axial stress is:

t = P·R / (S·E − 0.6·P)    (hoop)     t = P·R / (2·S·E + 0.4·P)    (axial)

For a sphere, and therefore for a hemispherical head, the membrane stress is the same in every direction and the thickness is:

t = P·R / (2·S·E − 0.2·P)

The corrosion allowance is added to the result, and R is the corroded radius. When the pressure is high compared with S, the thin-wall idea of a uniform stress breaks down. The thick-wall thickness comes from the Lamé solution:

Z = (S·E + P) / (S·E − P),    t = R·(√Z − 1)

Across the wall the hoop and radial stresses are:

σθ, σr = A ± B / r²,    A = (Pia² − Pob²)/(b² − a²),    B = (Pi − Po)a²b²/(b² − a²)

where a and b are the inside and outside radii. The largest hoop stress is always at the inside surface. For a bolted joint, the bolts must carry the end force and compress the gasket in service, and they must also seat the gasket at assembly:

H = 0.785·G²·P,   Hp = 2·b·π·G·m·P,   Wm1 = H + Hp,   Wm2 = π·b·G·y Am1 = Wm1/Sb,   Am2 = Wm2/Sa,   Am = max(Am1, Am2),   W = 0.5·(Am + Ab)·Sa

The ring flange is then loaded by the bolt load, the gasket reaction and the hydrostatic end forces. Each one multiplies its lever arm about the bolt circle to give a moment, and the tangential stress in the ring is proportional to that moment divided by the square of the thickness.

Understanding the Results

Required thickness and utilization

The required thickness is the minimum wall, including corrosion allowance, for the pressure you entered. Utilization is required divided by provided, so green is below 85%, amber is between 85% and 100%, and red means the wall is too thin.

Maximum allowable pressure

This is the pressure the thickness you provided can carry. It is useful for checking an existing vessel or for seeing how much margin a thicker wall buys.

Thin-wall error

In the thick-wall module, this is how much the thin-wall formula under-predicts the thickness for the same data. It grows quickly as the pressure approaches the allowable stress, which is why the simulator tells you when the thick-wall method is needed.

Am, Ab and the bolt load

Am is the bolt area the joint needs, and it is set by whichever is larger: the operating condition or the gasket seating condition. Ab is the area you provide. The joint passes when Ab is at least Am.

Flange stress ST

ST is the tangential stress in the flange ring in operation and at seating. It is compared with the flange allowable stress at the design and at ambient temperature.

Frequently Asked Questions

When can I treat a vessel as thin-walled?
As a rule of thumb, the thin-wall formulas are valid when the thickness is no more than half the inside radius and the pressure is below about 0.385 times S times E for a cylinder. Beyond that, the stress is no longer uniform through the wall and the Lamé thick-wall solution should be used. The simulator shows a note when you leave the thin-wall range.
Why is the hoop stress twice the axial stress in a cylinder?
Pressure pushes on the whole inside surface, but the hoop stress is carried by only two thin strips of wall along the length, while the axial stress is carried by the whole ring of wall at the end. The hoop stress is therefore PR/t and the axial stress is PR/2t, which is why a cylinder usually splits along its length first.
Why is a hemispherical head thinner than the cylinder?
A sphere has the same membrane stress in every direction, and it is half the hoop stress of a cylinder of the same radius, so it needs about half the wall. Flatter heads such as ellipsoidal and torispherical need more thickness because of bending near the knuckle, and a flat plate needs the most because it resists pressure in bending.
What do m and y mean for a gasket?
The factor m describes how much compressive load the gasket needs to keep sealing once the vessel is pressurized, as a multiple of the pressure. The seating stress y is the minimum stress needed to squeeze the gasket into the flange faces during assembly. Both depend on the gasket material and type, so enter the values given by your gasket data.
Why do I need two bolt checks?
The bolts have two jobs. In operation they carry the pressure end force and keep compression on the gasket, which gives Wm1. At assembly they must crush the gasket to seat it, which gives Wm2. The larger area governs, and with a soft gasket the seating condition often controls even when the operating load looks bigger.
What are the limitations of this simulator?
It covers internal pressure on shells and heads with the classic formulas, a Lamé model for thick cylinders with closed ends, and a constant-thickness ring flange. It does not model external pressure buckling, openings and nozzles, fatigue, temperature gradients, hubbed flanges, or the newer detailed flange methods. It is a learning and preliminary sizing tool, and a real vessel must be designed and certified under the code that applies to it.
