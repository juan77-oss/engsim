About this calculator

This calculator models two single-phase transformers operating in parallel — a common arrangement when one transformer can no longer supply a growing load, or when service continuity matters (if one unit fails, the other keeps supplying part of the load). From each transformer's nameplate and short-circuit test data it computes how the load is shared, the circulating current between the units, the maximum power the group can deliver without overloading either machine, and whether the parallel connection is acceptable at all.

How to use it

Enter each transformer's rated power (SN), its high- and low-voltage ratings, and its short-circuit test data: Ucc in volts, referred to the high-voltage winding, and Pcc in watts. Then set the group's total load with the slider, the power factor (inductive) and the working voltage U2. The catalog data is converted to percentage impedance (ucc%, ur%, ux%) automatically, and that conversion is shown in its own table so you can compare it with the ur% and ux% given in your own problem. To see a circulating current appear, change the low-voltage rating of one transformer by a few volts.

Understanding the results

The two status banners check the conditions that decide whether the parallel connection makes sense: the transformation ratios must match within 0.5%, and the circulating current must stay below 10% of the smaller transformer's rated current.

The loading bars show each winding's apparent power (U2 × I) as a percentage of its own rated power SN. Each bar is scaled independently, since a small transformer can reach 100% long before a large one does. A bar turns red at 100%, and it turns yellow between 90% and 100% only when the two loadings differ by more than 5 percentage points; two well-matched units can safely run close to their rating together. The load-share chart shows what fraction of the delivered load each transformer supplies. The per-transformer table gives each winding's current and phase angle and, when a circulating current is present, an extra row with the part of each current that actually reaches the load.

The group results give the circulating current IC (magnitude and angle), the maximum group power without overloading either machine, and which transformer is the limiting one (or a tie). In the phasor diagram, I1 and I2 are drawn against the reference voltage U2, and IC is the dashed segment joining the tips of I1 and I2: the difference the two currents would still have with the load removed.

How the load is shared

From catalog data to percentage impedance

ucc% = Ucc / UN,HV × 100 ur% = Pcc / (10 · SN[kVA]) ux% = √(ucc%² − ur%²) φcc = arctan(ux% / ur%)

Equal transformation ratios

With the same ratio, the currents divide inversely to the equivalent impedances, and the loading d = S / SN of each unit is inversely proportional to its ucc%:

I1 / I2 = Z2 / Z1 d1 · ucc1 = d2 · ucc2

The machine with the lowest ucc% carries the most load and reaches full load first. When rated powers and ucc% both differ, it is better for the smaller transformer to be the one with the higher ucc%, otherwise the smaller unit is the one that limits the group. The result does not depend on the size of the load, only on the impedances.

Different transformation ratios: circulating current

With k = UHV / ULV for each unit and Z the equivalent impedance referred to the secondary, the currents are:

I1 = [ k2·Z2·I + (k2 − k1)·U2 ] / ( k1·Z1 + k2·Z2 ) I2 = [ k1·Z1·I − (k2 − k1)·U2 ] / ( k1·Z1 + k2·Z2 ) IC = (k2 − k1)·U2 / ( k1·Z1 + k2·Z2 )

IC is the value the currents take with no load (I = 0). It adds to one winding and subtracts from the other, and it disappears when k1 = k2. Because the impedances are small, even a slight ratio mismatch produces a large circulating current.

Maximum group power

With equal ratios and in-phase currents, the unit with the lowest ucc% runs at d = 1 and the other at d = ucc,limiting / ucc,other:

Smax = SN,limiting + SN,other · (ucc,limiting / ucc,other)

The two currents are not exactly in phase when ur%/ux% differ, so the calculator solves the full phasor equations. Its value is therefore slightly lower than this shortcut.

Conditions for connecting transformers in parallel

ConditionTypeIf it is not met
Same frequencyMandatoryWith primaries on lines of different frequency the waveforms are no longer sinusoidal.
Same transformation ratio (within 0.5%)MandatoryA circulating current flows between the secondaries even at no load.
Same polarityMandatoryA voltmeter across the open connection reads about twice the rated voltage instead of zero; closing it would short-circuit the units.
Same connection group (three-phase)MandatoryThe phase displacement between secondaries differs and large short-circuit currents circulate.
Equal ucc%RecommendedThe load is not shared in proportion to the ratings, and the unit with the lowest ucc% reaches 100% first.
Equal ur% and ux%RecommendedThe two currents are out of phase, so the group can deliver less than the sum of the individual ratings.

A small ratio mismatch is tolerated in practice: a difference below 0.5%, or a circulating current not exceeding 10% of the rated current of either unit, is generally acceptable.

Worked example

Default values

T1: 5 kVA, Ucc = 345.85 V, Pcc = 133 W. T2: 2.3 kVA, Ucc = 285.75 V, Pcc = 62.1 W. Both 7620/231 V. Load: 6.5 kVA, power factor 0.8 inductive, U2 = 220 V.

Impedances: ucc% = 4.54% for T1 (ur = 2.66%, ux = 3.68%) and 3.75% for T2 (ur = 2.70%, ux = 2.60%).

Circulating current: both units have the same ratio, so IC = 0.

Currents: the load current is 6500 / 220 ≈ 29.5 A, split as I1 ≈ 19.05 A and I2 ≈ 10.60 A.

Loading: T1 supplies about 4.19 kVA (83.8% of its rating) and T2 about 2.33 kVA (101.4%). T2 has the lower ucc%, so it is the first to be overloaded and its bar turns red.

Maximum group power: about 6.41 kVA, limited by T2. The shortcut formula gives 2.3 + 5 × (3.75 / 4.54) ≈ 6.43 kVA. The 6.5 kVA load is therefore slightly above what the pair can carry.

Frequently asked questions

Why does circulating current exist even with no load connected?
When two transformers have different transformation ratios, their no-load secondary voltages differ slightly. Since both secondaries are tied together, that small voltage difference drives a current through the internal impedances — which are small — so even a tiny mismatch produces a noticeable circulating current.
What happens if I connect transformers of different power ratings in parallel?
It's allowed as long as the eligibility conditions are met. The load then splits inversely proportional to each transformer's percentage impedance (ucc%) — the transformer with the lower ucc% reaches its nominal rating first, regardless of which one is physically larger.
Why is the catalog short-circuit voltage (Ucc) referred to the primary winding?
The short-circuit test is normally run by shorting the low-voltage winding and applying a reduced voltage to the high-voltage winding, since that side draws lower, easier-to-measure currents. That's why manufacturer datasheets list Ucc referred to the HV (primary) side.
What conditions must two transformers meet to operate in parallel?
Four conditions are mandatory: the same frequency, the same transformation ratio (within about 0.5%), the same polarity and, for three-phase units, the same connection group. Two more do not forbid the connection but improve load sharing: equal percentage short-circuit voltages (ucc%) and equal resistive and reactive components (ur% and ux%).
Which transformer overloads first when two units are in parallel?
The one with the lower percentage short-circuit voltage (ucc%). The load divides inversely to ucc%, so that unit reaches 100% of its rating first. The calculator reports it as the limiting transformer and turns its loading bar red at 100%.
How is the maximum power of a parallel group calculated?
The load is raised until the first transformer reaches its rated value. With equal ratios and in-phase currents, the limiting unit runs at 100% and the other at ucc,limiting / ucc,other of its rating, so Smax = SN,limiting + SN,other × (ucc,limiting / ucc,other). Because the two currents are never exactly in phase, the calculator solves the full phasor equations, which gives a slightly lower value than this shortcut.
